Sydney's Story Map

The question

My site is Sydney, Australia, and my story map argues that understanding Sydney's urban environment requires looking beyond individual maps because environmental patterns and infrastructure networks are shaped by different spatial boundaries and scales. I want to examine how the pattern and distribution of Sydney's natural environment relate to urban development and infrastructure. More importantly, I want to understand the relationship between these maps without assuming that they share the same spatial unit.

The data

The method

I selected five maps: Terrain, Vegetation, Watershed, Land Cover, and Infrastructure. I set aside Climate because the Sydney Airport station provides only a limited representation of the city's climate, while the climate map does not directly strengthen the central argument about the relationship between environmental space and urban infrastructure. I also set aside Table-to-Map because its country-level administrative polygons operate at a different scale and do not directly demonstrate Sydney's urban-environmental relationships. The sequence follows The Escalating Claim. Terrain establishes the physical foundation. Vegetation then shows ecological variation within that landscape. Watershed expands the argument to a natural system that crosses ordinary administrative boundaries. Land Cover introduces human transformation of the surface. Finally, Infrastructure raises the stakes by showing the networks through which urban activities are supported. The maps do not share one mapping unit. Terrain uses raster cells, while Vegetation combines green-space boundaries with raster cells. Watershed uses a delineated catchment, Land Cover uses classified raster cells, and Infrastructure uses a user-defined bounding box. These changes matter because each map describes a different spatial system rather than an identical area.

My evidence is the published ArcGIS StoryMap. Each map is embedded from the corresponding web map published during Weeks 2–8. The captions identify the data source, mapping unit, and relevant boundary change.

What I found

The sequence suggests that Sydney's urban environment cannot be understood through a single spatial pattern. Its physical terrain provides the setting for ecological variation and water movement, while land-cover classification reveals the transformation of the surface by human activity. Infrastructure adds another layer: urban networks occupy and connect spaces that do not necessarily follow the same boundaries as natural systems. Putting these maps together therefore reveals a relationship between natural processes, land transformation, and the infrastructure supporting urban life. The changing mapping units are not merely technical details; they influence what becomes visible and how we interpret the distribution of features across Sydney.

What this does not show

The story map does not establish that land-cover change directly caused infrastructure expansion, or that the mapped infrastructure is equally accessible to all residents. A useful missing map would show population distribution and access to infrastructure in relation to environmental risks, such as flooding or heat. Without this evidence, I cannot make a strong claim about who benefits from infrastructure or who is most exposed to environmental pressures.
