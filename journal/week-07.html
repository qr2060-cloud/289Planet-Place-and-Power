Sydney's Land Cover

The question

This journal explores the relationship between Australia's international trade and its physical geography. I focus on the scale and distribution of exports in 2024. My main question is: How can trade data help us understand Australia's global economic connections, and what can a map reveal—or conceal—about the physical places supporting these connections?

The data

I used two 2024 datasets from the Atlas of Economic Complexity: Australia's exports by destination and its exports by product. In the product dataset, iron ores and concentrates were the largest listed category, worth approximately US$74.24 billion, or 19.67% of the recorded total. Coal was the second-largest category. In the destination dataset, China was the largest destination, receiving approximately US$106.58 billion, equivalent to 36.36% of the recorded total. I also used Natural Earth's Admin 0 – Countries dataset, version 5.1.1, to provide country boundaries for the world map. 1. Export by destination (gross export value): I am using each destination country's gross export value to stand for the economic importance of Australia's trade with that country, and it might fail because the value records trade destinations rather than the specific places where the exported goods were produced. 2. Export by product: I am using the gross export value of iron ores and concentrates to stand for the economic importance of Australia's iron-ore industry, and it might fail because export value does not show the physical area occupied by mines or the environmental impacts of extraction. 3. Natural Earth country boundaries: I am using country polygons to stand for the geographical units used to map Australia's export destinations, and it might fail because some trade destinations or territories may not correspond exactly to the countries represented by the boundary dataset. 4. Sydney transport locations: I am using the locations of Port Botany, Sydney Airport, and Enfield Intermodal Logistics Centre to stand for Sydney's trade-related transport infrastructure, and it might fail because three selected locations cannot represent the full distribution or capacity of the city's freight and transport networks.

The method

I uploaded the destination CSV as a table and the Natural Earth country boundaries as a feature layer in ArcGIS Online. I then joined the destination data to the country polygons using country names and displayed exports_usd. with the “Counts and Amounts (color)” style. The resulting choropleth uses different shades of blue to represent different export-value ranges. I also imported a CSV containing three Sydney transport locations as a point layer: Port Botany, Sydney Airport, and Enfield Intermodal Logistics Centre.

What I found

I am using national gross export value to stand for the geographical distribution of Australia's export-related economic activity, and it might fail because trade values are attributed to destination countries rather than to the specific mines, factories, ports, and transport networks responsible for producing and moving goods. Similarly, three Sydney transport locations cannot represent all of Australia's trade infrastructure. These maps reveal useful spatial patterns, but they do not show the complete physical supply chain or the land area occupied by export production.
