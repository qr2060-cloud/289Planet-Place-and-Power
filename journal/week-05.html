Sydney's Water

The question

Where does water at my Sydney site come from and go to, and who else has claims over it? I investigate how connectivity, proximity, and adjacency shape the relationship between natural drainage, urban infrastructure, and water stress. I compare the modeled catchment with a water-stress polygon to examine whether these spatial boundaries describe the same water system.

The data

I used Global Watersheds to delineate the upstream catchment. The comparison uses MERIT-Hydro (2021) at lower and higher precision and HydroSHEDS (2013) at lower precision. I also used the World Resources Institute’s Aqueduct 4.0 Water Risk layer in ArcGIS Online. Its baseline water-stress data represent 1979–2019, and the dataset is licensed under CC BY 4.0. Catchment area is measured in square kilometres (km²); water stress is recorded as a ratio and a categorical class.

The method

The watershed model uses terrain and river-network information to identify upstream drainage. My mapped result delineated a catchment of 390 km², with an outlet near −33.853, 151.202. The reported snapping distance was 3.2 km, showing that the model’s snapped river outlet did not exactly match the requested point. Across the three comparison runs, the areas were 437 km² (MERIT-Hydro, lower precision), 370 km² (MERIT-Hydro, higher precision), and 263 km² (HydroSHEDS, lower precision). The spread was approximately 1.7×. The water-stress polygon covers 3,863 km², so my 390 km² catchment is about 10.1% of its area.

What I found

Sydney’s baseline annual water-stress value is 0.627, classified as High (40–80%). Monthly values range from 0.39 in January to 1.18 in June, meaning the annual average conceals substantial seasonal variation. The catchment estimates also differ: the largest is 1.7 times the smallest, so the delineated boundary depends partly on the dataset and precision used. One infrastructure feature complicates the natural drainage picture: the Northside Storage Tunnel. Sydney Water’s project information describes a tunnel system that conveys wastewater toward the North Head treatment system, illustrating how engineered infrastructure can move water across natural drainage boundaries. The system is approximately 20 km long, with a storage capacity of 225 ML. This is a different kind of connectivity from surface runoff.

What this does not show

I am using the modeled catchment to stand for the area contributing surface runoff, and it might fail because underground sewers and engineered transfers can cross the topographic divide. Similarly, the water-stress index describes a broader basin-scale pressure, not the amount of usable water available at my exact site. The mismatch between the 390 km² catchment and the 3,863 km² stress polygon means the two maps should not be interpreted as equivalent boundaries.
