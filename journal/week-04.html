Sydney's Vegetation

The question

How do pattern, scale, and density of vegetation across my Sydney site affect what NDVI identifies as “green” space? I want to test whether areas that appear highly green at the landscape scale also represent places with substantial ecological habitat.

The data

I used Landsat Explorer with a Landsat 8 OLI/TIRS scene acquired on August 21, 2026. Each pixel is 30 m × 30 m (900 m²), and the scene has 1% cloud cover. The data are publicly available. I used NDVI (Normalized Difference Vegetation Index), calculated from Band 4 (red) and Band 5 (near-infrared). NDVI uses the contrast between vegetation absorbing red light and reflecting near-infrared light.

Write the proxy sentence here, once per indicator: I am using NDVI to stand for the amount of living green vegetation, and it might fail because different kinds of vegetation and managed surfaces can produce similar index values.

The method

I used the NDVI Raw rendering to obtain numerical values and NDVI Colorized to visualize the spatial pattern. I applied the tutorial’s 0.5 threshold, treating pixels with NDVI ≥ 0.5 as “green,” and also tested 0.4 and 0.6 to see whether changing the threshold improved the ecological interpretation. I used the colorized map to compare the pattern and density of green areas across Sydney.

What I found

My Part 0 prediction ranked Royal National Park first, Ku-ring-gai Chase National Park second, and Sydney Olympic Garden third. My final NDVI results were broadly consistent with my expectation: Royal National Park = 0.85, Ku-ring-gai Chase National Park = 0.71, and Sydney Olympic Park = 0.40. The main difference is that the actual results included Ku-ring-gai Chase National Park and Sydney Olympic Park rather than the two Botanic Garden locations I predicted. This suggests that the broad ranking of greener versus less-green environments was more reliable than predicting the exact places.

What this does not show

The 5 m cell size limits what this map can show. Features smaller than approximately one cell, such as small drainage channels, kerbs, road edges, and small embankments, may be averaged or omitted from the raster. Therefore, the map cannot by itself show the exact path that water takes through the site. The slope map also does not show whether a slope is structurally stable. Slope is derived from elevation, while stability would require additional information about materials, water pressure, vegetation, and other physical conditions. The elevation data also have limitations related to their metadata, including the stated or unstated vertical accuracy, vertical datum, and the dates of the underlying surveys. The dates of the elevation data should not automatically be interpreted as the dates when the current landscape or buildings were constructed. The soil layer has a different limitation: SoilGrids represents a prediction, not direct measurement at every location. Its uncertainty therefore matters when interpreting the spatial pattern. Finally, the rendering itself is not neutral. Changing the classification breaks can make certain slope differences more visually prominent. I therefore need to state the symbology and classification used rather than presenting the visual pattern as if it were simply the raw data
